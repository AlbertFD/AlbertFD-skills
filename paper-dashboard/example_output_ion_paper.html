01What this paper achieved

The ASACUSA collaboration at CERN reported the first production of cold, long-lived antiprotonic helium ions — the two-body system p̄He²⁺, an antiproton bound to a bare helium nucleus — surviving ~100 ns, long enough to open the door to ultra-precise laser spectroscopy.

From a three-body atom to a two-body ion

Ordinary antiprotonic helium is a three-body atom, p̄He⁺ ≡ p̄ + He²⁺ + e⁻: an antiproton and an electron both orbiting the helium nucleus. Strip away the electron and you are left with a two-body ion, p̄He²⁺ — a pure Coulomb system.

Two-body systems are a theorist's paradise: their energy levels can be computed essentially exactly. The catch had always been that such ions are destroyed within picoseconds. This experiment solved that.

Why it matters

The energy levels of a two-body ion follow a near-perfect Bohr formula, calculable to ~10⁻⁸ with negligible relativistic and QED corrections — making p̄He²⁺ a potentially better ruler than the three-body atom for measuring the antiproton's mass and charge.

Comparing antiproton and proton properties is a direct test of CPT symmetry — the requirement that matter and antimatter obey mirror-image physics.

The four headline results

1 · Long-lived ions exist

Cold p̄⁴He²⁺ and p̄³He²⁺ ions in circular states (ni=28–32, ℓi=ni−1) live on the 100-ns scale — orders of magnitude longer than the picosecond fate of hot ions.

2 · A clean two-step recipe

Cool the neutral three-body atom, then fire a laser to drive it to an auto-ionizing state; a spontaneous Auger decay ejects the electron and leaves a cold ion in a known quantum state.

3 · Isotope & state dependence

p̄³He²⁺ ions die faster than p̄⁴He²⁺ (smaller reduced mass → faster collisions), and decay rates grow with the quantum number ni.

4 · A path to spectroscopy

The measured cross section (σ ≈ 4–10 × 10⁻¹⁵ cm²) and the ni-dependence of lifetimes suggest laser spectroscopy between ionic states is feasible.

02The physics, equation by equation

Two equations carry the argument: one says why the ion is a beautiful test system (its energy levels), the other says how it dies (collisional annihilation). A third describes the two-step production scheme.

Equation 1 — the energy levels of an ideal Bohr system

Because \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^{2+}\) is a pure two-body Coulomb bound state, its spin-independent energy levels follow a Bohr-like formula evaluable to a fractional precision of ~10⁻⁸:

\[ E_n = -\,\frac{4\,R_\infty\,h\,c}{n_i^{2}}\;\frac{M}{m_e}\;\frac{Q_{\overline p}^{2}}{e^{2}} \]
EnBinding energy of level n_i (negative = bound).
R∞Rydberg constant — the master energy scale of Coulomb binding.
h·cConverts the Rydberg (inverse length) into an energy.
ni2Principal quantum number squared — levels crowd as n_i grows.
M/meReduced mass in electron-mass units; the "4" and this ratio replace the electron with the heavy antiproton orbiting He.
Qp̄²/e²Antiproton charge squared in units of e² — encodes the nuclear charge product.

Why this is the whole point

For atomic hydrogen's 2p state, relativistic corrections enter at ~10⁻⁵ and QED at ~10⁻⁶, muddying any high-precision comparison. In \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^{2+}\) these are far smaller because the antiproton is heavy with a tiny magnetic moment and its orbit is circular, barely overlapping the nucleus. The level structure is a ruler trustworthy to ~10⁻⁸, set only by R_∞, M and Q_p̄.

Equation 2 — how collisions kill the ion

In a gas, a collision Stark-mixes the clean circular state into low-ℓ states (S, P, D) that overlap the nucleus. Modeled as binary collisions with He, the decay rate rises linearly with target density ρ:

\[ \gamma_i(\rho) \;\simeq\; \gamma_i(0) \;+\; \sigma\,\rho\,\sqrt{\dfrac{8\,k_B T}{\pi\,M_\mathrm{red}}} \]
γi(ρ)Total decay rate at density ρ (inverse of observed lifetime).
γi(0)Intrinsic rate of an isolated ion (radiative cascade limit).
σCross section for collision-induced annihilation — what the experiment extracts.
ρHe target number density. Lower ρ → fewer collisions → longer life.
√(8kBT/πMred)Mean relative ion–He speed: the kinetic factor in the collision rate.
MredReduced mass of the ion–He system; smaller (³He) → faster death.

The key prediction

The decay rate is a straight line in density with slope ∝ σ√(T/M_red). Driving density low enough reveals the ion's own intrinsic lifetime as the intercept γ_i(0). Earlier experiments ran 1–2 orders of magnitude too dense to see it; this one reached ρ ~ 3×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and finally did.

Equation 3 — the two-step production scheme

The recipe that makes cold ions in a known state rather than hot ones that vanish instantly:

\[ \overline p\mathrm{He}^{+}_{(n,\ell)} \;\xrightarrow{\;h\nu\;}\; \overline p\mathrm{He}^{+}_{(n_A,\ell_A)} \;\longrightarrow\; \overline p\mathrm{He}^{2+}_{(n_i,\ell_i)} + e^- \]
1

Thermalize

The neutral three-body atom survives long enough to cool to the 10 K gas (its electron blocks Stark mixing).

2

Laser kick

A photon hν drives it to an Auger-dominated state (n_A, ℓ_A).

3

Auger decay

The atom ejects its electron, leaving a cold ion p̄He²⁺ in the nearest ionic state.

03The experiment

The setup resembles earlier neutral-atom work, but pushed to far lower densities and finer timing so the ion's own lifetime could finally outshine the Auger background.

The central problem: hot ions die in picoseconds

Antiprotons stopping in helium make p̄He²⁺ ions directly once their energy drops below ~10 eV — but those ions recoil at an effective temperature T ~ 10⁴ K. A few collisions Stark-mix the high-ℓ state into S/P/D states that overlap the nucleus, and the ion annihilates within picoseconds. To study ions by laser spectroscopy you need them cold and in a known, long-lived state — hence the two-step scheme.

The apparatus, stage by stage

A

Antiproton Decelerator (AD), CERN

Delivers pulses of 3×10⁷ antiprotons at E = 5.3 MeV, ~100 ns long, every ~100 s (f = 0.01 Hz).

B

RFQ decelerator (RFQD)

Slows ~30% of each pulse to T ≈ 80 keV; an achromatic analyzer and a thin (1.3 µm) polyethylene-naphthalate window let antiprotons enter the gas.

C

Ultra-low-density helium target

A 15-cm-diameter, 30-cm-long chamber of ⁴He (99.9999%) or ³He (99.997%) at T ≈ 10 K, at densities as low as ρ = 3×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ (~3×10⁻² mbar).

D

High-power tunable laser

A Nd:YAG-pumped dye laser produced 2–4 ns pulses tunable over λ = 264–726 nm at 10–80 mJ/pulse, expanded to a 12 cm beam.

E

Cherenkov detection

Charged pions from annihilation were caught by Cherenkov counters on a microchannel-plate PMT with sub-ns resolution — a delayed annihilation time spectrum.

The cold-ion check & the detection trick

Ion temperature was inferred from the thermal Doppler width (Γ < 500 MHz) of the 597.3 nm resonance. Because the ejected Auger electron carries only ~0.8 eV (recoil ~100 µeV), the daughter ion stays as cold as its parent (~10 K). As density drops, the annihilation "spike" tail lengthens beyond the Auger lifetime — the unambiguous fingerprint of cold, long-lived ions.

What the numbers add up to

From the measured rates and Eq. 2 the collisional cross section is σ ≈ 4–10 × 10⁻¹⁵ cm² — an order of magnitude larger than theoretical Stark-mixing cross sections, though those were computed for hot (10⁴ K) recoiling ions and can't be compared directly. The clear nᵢ-dependence of the lifetimes is itself the proposed handle for detecting laser resonances between ionic states.
