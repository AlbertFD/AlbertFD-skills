01What this paper achieved

The ASACUSA collaboration at CERN reported the first production of cold, long-lived antiprotonic helium ions — the two-body system p̄He²⁺, an antiproton bound to a bare helium nucleus — and showed they survive ~100 ns, long enough to open the door to ultra-precise laser spectroscopy.

From a three-body atom to a two-body ion

Ordinary antiprotonic helium is a three-body atom, p̄He⁺ ≡ p̄ + He²⁺ + e⁻: an antiproton and an electron both orbiting the helium nucleus. Strip away the electron and you are left with a two-body ion, p̄He²⁺ — just the antiproton orbiting the nucleus, a pure Coulomb system.

Two-body systems are theorists' paradise: their energy levels can be computed essentially exactly. The catch had always been that such ions are destroyed within picoseconds. This experiment solved that.

Why it matters

The energy levels of a two-body ion follow a near-perfect Bohr formula, calculable to ~10⁻⁸ with negligible relativistic and QED corrections. That makes p̄He²⁺ a potentially better ruler than the three-body atom for measuring the antiproton's mass and charge.

Comparing antiproton and proton properties is a direct test of CPT symmetry — the deep requirement that matter and antimatter obey mirror-image physics.

The four headline results

1 · Long-lived ions exist

Cold p̄⁴He²⁺ and p̄³He²⁺ ions in circular states (ni=28–32, ℓi=ni−1) live on the 100-ns scale — orders of magnitude longer than the picosecond fate of hot ions.

2 · A clean two-step recipe

Cool the neutral three-body atom, then fire a laser to drive it to an auto-ionizing state; a spontaneous Auger decay ejects the electron and leaves a cold ion in a known quantum state.

3 · Isotope & state dependence

p̄³He²⁺ ions die faster than p̄⁴He²⁺ (smaller reduced mass → faster collisions), and decay rates grow with the quantum number ni.

4 · A path to spectroscopy

The measured collisional cross section (σ ≈ 4–10 × 10⁻¹⁵ cm²) and the ni-dependence of lifetimes suggest laser spectroscopy between ionic states is feasible — a prediction later borne out (see References).

02The physics, equation by equation

Two equations carry the paper's argument: one says why the ion is a beautiful test system (its energy levels), the other says how it dies (collisional annihilation). We unpack each term, then look at the energy-level ladder of Fig. 1.

What exactly is \(\overline{p}\mathrm{He}^{2+}\)?

The neutral parent is a three-body atomcule: an antiproton (\(\overline p\)) and an electron (\(e^-\)) both bound to a helium nucleus (He²⁺), written \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^+ \equiv \overline p + \mathrm{He}^{2+} + e^-\). The antiproton sits in a highly excited, near-circular orbit (principal quantum number \(n \approx 38\)), which is why it survives microseconds: the orbit barely overlaps the nucleus, so annihilation is slow.

Remove the electron and you have the antiprotonic helium ion \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^{2+}\): a single antiproton orbiting a bare He nucleus. This is a hydrogen-like, two-body Coulomb problem — but with an antiproton (mass ~1836 \(m_e\)) playing the role of the "electron." The states populated here have \(n_i = 28\text{–}32\) and the maximal angular momentum \(\ell_i = n_i - 1\) ("circular" or "yrast" states).

Equation 1 — the energy levels of an ideal Bohr system

Because \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^{2+}\) is a pure two-body Coulomb bound state, its spin-independent energy levels follow a Bohr-like formula that can be evaluated to a fractional precision of ~10⁻⁸:

\[ E_n = -\,\frac{4\,R_\infty\,h\,c}{n_i^{2}}\;\frac{M}{m_e}\;\frac{Q_{\overline p}^{2}}{e^{2}} \]

Each factor has a clear physical meaning — hover any card:

EnBinding energy of level \(n_i\) (negative = bound).
R∞Rydberg constant — the master energy scale of Coulomb binding.
h·cConverts the Rydberg (an inverse length) into an energy.
ni2Principal quantum number squared — levels crowd together as \(n_i\) grows.
M / meReduced mass in electron-mass units. The "4" and this ratio replace the electron with the heavy antiproton orbiting He.
Qp̄² / e²Antiproton charge squared in units of \(e^2\) — encodes the nuclear charge product.

Why this is the whole point

For atomic hydrogen's 2p state, relativistic corrections enter at ~10⁻⁵ and QED corrections at ~10⁻⁶, muddying any high-precision comparison. In \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^{2+}\) these are far smaller because (i) the antiproton is heavy and has a tiny magnetic moment, and (ii) its semiclassical orbit is circular, with almost no overlap with the nucleus. The level structure is therefore "clean" — a ruler you can trust to ~10⁻⁸, set only by \(R_\infty\), \(M\), and \(Q_{\overline p}\).

The radiative cascade and the lifetime of an isolated ion

Left entirely alone, the ion does not live forever: the antiproton steps down through a ladder of radiative transitions of the type \(\Delta n_i = \Delta \ell_i = -1\) (the straight arrows in Fig. 1), staying circular as it falls. When it reaches \(n_i \approx 3\) its orbit finally overlaps the nucleus and it is absorbed (annihilates). A simple estimate gives an isolated lifetime near \(n_i = 30\) of

\[ \tau_i \approx 0.3\text{–}0.4\ \mu\mathrm{s} \]

So even a perfectly isolated ion lives only a fraction of a microsecond — but that is plenty of time for pulsed laser spectroscopy. The experimental challenge was never the radiative lifetime; it was collisions.

Equation 3 — how collisions kill the ion

In a gas, the ion is destroyed when a collision Stark-mixes its clean circular state into low-\(\ell\) states (S, P, D) that do overlap the nucleus. Modeling this as binary collisions with He atoms, the decay rate rises linearly with target density \(\rho\):

\[ \gamma_i(\rho) \;\simeq\; \gamma_i(0) \;+\; \sigma\,\rho\,\sqrt{\dfrac{8\,k_B T}{\pi\,M_\mathrm{red}}} \]
γi(ρ)Total decay rate at density \(\rho\) (inverse of the observed lifetime).
γi(0)Intrinsic rate of an isolated ion (the radiative cascade limit, ~few hundred ns).
σCross section for collision-induced annihilation — what the experiment extracts.
ρHe target number density. Lower \(\rho\) → fewer collisions → longer life.
√(8kBT/πMred)Mean relative speed of the ion–He pair: the kinetic factor in the collision rate.
MredReduced mass of the ion–He system. Smaller \(M_\mathrm{red}\) (³He) → faster speed → faster death.

The key prediction

Equation 3 says the decay rate should be a straight line in density with slope \( \mathrm{d}\gamma_i/\mathrm{d}\rho \propto \sigma\sqrt{T/M_\mathrm{red}} \). Driving the density low enough should reveal the ion's own intrinsic lifetime as the line's intercept \(\gamma_i(0)\). Earlier experiments ran 1–2 orders of magnitude too dense to see it — this one reached \(\rho \sim 3\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\) and finally did.

Equation 2 — the two-step production scheme

The recipe that makes cold ions in a known state, rather than hot ones that vanish instantly:

\[ \overline p\mathrm{He}^{+}_{(n,\ell)} \;\xrightarrow{\;h\nu\;}\; \overline p\mathrm{He}^{+}_{(n_A,\ell_A)} \;\longrightarrow\; \overline p\mathrm{He}^{2+}_{(n_i,\ell_i)} + e^- \]
1

Thermalize

The neutral three-body atom survives long enough to cool to the 10 K gas (its electron blocks Stark mixing).

2

Laser kick

A photon \(h\nu\) drives it to an Auger-dominated state \((n_A,\ell_A)\).

3

Auger decay

The atom spontaneously ejects its electron, leaving a cold ion \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^{2+}_{(n_i,\ell_i)}\) in the nearest ionic state.

03The experiment

The setup resembles earlier neutral-atom work, but pushed to far lower densities and finer timing so the ion's own lifetime could finally outshine the Auger background. Here is the chain from antiproton beam to annihilation signal.

The central problem this had to beat

Hot ions die in picoseconds

Antiprotons stopping in helium make \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^{2+}\) ions directly in doubly-ionizing collisions once their energy drops below ~10 eV — but those ions recoil at an effective temperature \(T \sim 10^4\) K. A handful of collisions Stark-mix the high-\(\ell\) state into S/P/D states that overlap the nucleus, and the ion annihilates within picoseconds. To study ions by laser spectroscopy you instead need them cold and in a known, long-lived state — hence the two-step scheme.

The apparatus, stage by stage

A

Antiproton Decelerator (AD), CERN

Delivers pulses of \(3\times10^{7}\) antiprotons at \(E = 5.3\) MeV, ~100 ns long, every ~100 s (f = 0.01 Hz). Careful tuning of the AD electron cooler minimized energy spread and emittance.

B

Radio-frequency quadrupole decelerator (RFQD)

Slows ~30% of each pulse to \(T \approx 80\) keV. An achromatic analyzer (dipole + quadrupole + solenoid magnets) and a thin polyethylene-naphthalate window (1.3 µm) let the antiprotons enter the gas.

C

Ultra-low-density helium target

A 15-cm-diameter, 30-cm-long chamber of ⁴He (99.9999%) or ³He (99.997%) at \(T \approx 10\) K. Antiprotons were stopped and \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^+\) formed at densities as low as \(\rho = 3\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\) (pressure ~3×10⁻² mbar), measured by capacitance manometers.

D

High-power tunable laser

A Nd:YAG-pumped dye laser (four amplifier stages) produced 2–4 ns pulses tunable over \(\lambda = 264\text{–}726\) nm at 10–80 mJ/pulse, expanded to a 12 cm beam to flood the whole stopping volume.

E

Cherenkov detection

Charged pions from antiproton annihilation were caught by Cherenkov counters read out by a microchannel-plate photomultiplier with sub-nanosecond resolution, recorded on a digital oscilloscope as a delayed annihilation time spectrum.

The detection trick: reading the tail of the "spike"

What a spike is

When the laser drives the atom to an Auger-dominated state, a burst of annihilations — a "spike" — appears. The spike's tail normally measures the Auger lifetime \(\tau^A_{(n_A,\ell_A)}\) directly, because any ion made by the Auger process is immediately destroyed by Stark mixing.

The signature of success

As the density drops, the ion's own lifetime starts to compete with the Auger lifetime. The tail lengthens beyond \(\tau^A\). That prolongation is the unambiguous fingerprint of cold, long-lived ions — exactly what Fig. 2 shows when density falls 100-fold.

The cold-ion check

Ion temperature was inferred from the thermal Doppler width (\(\Gamma < 500\) MHz) of the 597.3 nm \(\overline p\mathrm{He}^+\) resonance. Because the ejected Auger electron carries only ~0.8 eV — giving the ion a recoil of just ~100 µeV — the daughter ion stays essentially as cold as its parent atom (~10 K). Out of ~10¹⁰ stopped antiprotons, roughly 10⁶ such ions were formed per low-density spectrum.

What the numbers add up to

From the measured rates and Eq. 3 the collisional cross section is \(\sigma \approx 4\text{–}10 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) — an order of magnitude larger than theoretical Stark-mixing cross sections, though those were computed for hot (\(10^4\) K) recoiling ions and can't be compared directly with these cold ions. The decay rates are consistent with isolated-ion lifetimes of a few hundred nanoseconds. The clear nᵢ-dependence of the lifetimes is itself the proposed handle for detecting laser resonances between ionic states.
