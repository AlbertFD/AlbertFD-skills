Eigenvectors — finally seeing what they are

An interactive lesson built around the specific thing that was tripping you up.

What was actually going wrong

You can compute \(Av\) and grind out the determinant fine — none of that is broken. The missing piece sits underneath: you've been experiencing a matrix as a rule for producing a new vector rather than as a transformation that picks up the whole plane and moves it. Eigenvectors feel hollow because "directions that don't get knocked off their line" means nothing until you can feel the plane turning in the first place. This page makes the plane move so you can see it.

1. First, let your old model fail

Here's a matrix that rotates the plane by 90°: $$R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$$ Predict before revealing: is there any nonzero vector \(v\) that \(R\) leaves pointing along its own line (i.e. \(Rv=\lambda v\) for some real number \(\lambda\))?

No — none. Every direction gets turned a quarter turn, so nothing stays on its own line. If you think of the matrix as "just a multiplication rule," that fact is invisible. If you think of it as rotating the plane, it's obvious: a rotation has no invariant directions (over the reals). That picture — "what does this matrix do to the plane?" — is the whole game. Play with it below.

3. Re-read the definition, now that it means something

The equation you'd memorised: $$A v = \lambda v$$ In English, with the picture you now have: "\(A\) moves the whole plane, but the arrow \(v\) doesn't get turned off its own line — it only gets scaled by \(\lambda\)." The eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is just how much that special direction gets stretched (\(\lambda>1\)), squashed (\(0<\lambda<1\)), or flipped (\(\lambda<0\)). That's why people care: eigenvectors are the axes along which a complicated transformation becomes simple multiplication.

4. Practice — spot the trap

5. Transfer challenge

A matrix you haven't seen here: $$M=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$$ Which of these is an eigenvector of \(M\)? Decide using the picture (does \(Mv\) stay on \(v\)'s line?), then reveal.

Show the worked reasoning

\(M\) is upper-triangular, so its eigenvalues are the diagonal: \(\lambda=3\) and \(\lambda=2\). For \(\lambda=3\): \((M-3I)v=0 \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}v=0 \Rightarrow v=(1,0)\). Check: \(M(1,0)=(3,0)=3\cdot(1,0)\) ✓ — stays on its line. For \(\lambda=2\): \(v=(1,-1)\), and \(M(1,-1)=(2,-2)=2\cdot(1,-1)\) ✓. The vector \((1,1)\) is the tempting trap: \(M(1,1)=(4,2)\), which points a different way — it got turned, so it is not an eigenvector even though it looks "neat."
